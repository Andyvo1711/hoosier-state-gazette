A client recently shared an experience at Queen Nails Bar & Spa Illinois that began like any ordinary visit. She noted that she had been to the salon before and "had good experiences," which says a lot about the team's ability to earn repeat business. This time, however, the visit ended with her leaving before any service started. Her short account carries a clear message about the first few minutes of a client's visit.

## Seated, but Not Served

The client says she asked for a pedicure and was told to sit in chair 2. After that, she writes, "not a single person came over." At the time, she observed one client getting a pedicure and roughly six employees seated on their devices. She kept looking toward them, and they looked back at her, which she described as awkward. Being directed to a chair feels like a welcome, but it also creates an expectation that someone will follow within a minute or two. When no one does, the silence is easy to read as indifference, even when the team simply did not realize she was waiting. A simple routine can prevent this: whoever seats a guest also tells her who will be helping her and roughly when, and a team member checks in with her if more than a few minutes pass.

## Quiet Moments Are Part of the Service

A slow period is a natural part of a salon's day, and a team is entitled to a break. Still, clients arriving during a lull see the room as they find it. Staff who are seated and looking at their phones can give an impression of disengagement, whatever the reason. Many salons set a simple expectation for quiet times, such as keeping eyes up toward the door, greeting every guest with a smile, and using the time to tidy stations, restock supplies, or prepare for the next appointment. A team member who is assigned to welcome walk-ins during slower hours helps make sure that no one is left in a chair on their own.

## Acknowledging a Client Who Leaves

The client says she put her shoes on and walked out, and "no one said a word." Even at that point, there was still a chance to recover. A quick "Is everything okay?" or "We are so sorry, we can take you right now" might have changed the outcome. When a client leaves unhappy, a friendly word at the door shows that the salon cares, and it opens the chance for her to explain what went wrong or return another day.

## Editorial Perspective

The lessons here are easy to put into practice. First, greet every guest as soon as she walks in, and make sure someone owns each person who is seated, with a short check-in if she waits. Second, treat quiet periods as time to stay engaged and ready, with phones kept away from the floor, so the room feels welcoming to anyone who arrives. Third, always acknowledge a client who is leaving, with a warm word and an offer to help, because a returning client who has had good experiences before is one of a salon's most valuable relationships.
